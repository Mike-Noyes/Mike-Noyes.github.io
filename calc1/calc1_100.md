# The Calc 1 100

Here are the problems.  Chip away at them.  They are roughly in the order in which the material is presented in a typical Calc 1 course.  The level of difficulty varies, but they should all be solvable using the tools and techniques learned in a Calc 1 course.

## The Problems

1. Suppose that a function $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[0,1]$ and that $0\leq f(x)\leq 1$ for every $x$ in $[0,1]$.  Show that there must exist a number $c$ in $[0,1]$ such that $f(c) = c$ ($c$ is called a **fixed point** of $f$).

1. *The Dirichlet Ruler Function:* If $x$ is a rational number, then $x$ can be written in a unique way as the quotient of integers $m/n$ where $n>0$ and $m$ and $n$ have no common factors greater than $1$.  We say that such a fraction is in *lowest terms*.  Let $f(x)$ be defined for all $x$ in the interval $[0,1]$ by

    $$ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1/n & \text{if } x =m/n \text{ is a rational number in lowest terms} \\ 0   & \text{if } x  \text{ is irrational} \end{cases} $$

    1. Show that $f$ is discontinuous at every rational number in $[0,1]$.
    2. Show that $f$ is continuous at every irrational number in $[0,1]$.
    3. Sketch the graph of $f$.  Why do you think $f$ is called the *ruler function*?


1. Suppose that the functions $f$ and $g$ are defined throughout an open interval containing the point $x_0$, that $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, that $f(x_0)=0$, and that $g$ is continuous at $x_0$.  Show that the product $fg$ is differentiable at $x_0$.  This shows, for example, that while $|x|$ is not differentiable at $x=0$, the product $x|x|$ *is* differentiable at $x=0$.
 



1. Suppose that a function $f$ satisfies the following conditions for all real values of $x$ and $y$:
   * $f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)$
   * $f(x)=1+g(x)$ where $\lim_{x\to 0} g(x) =1$
   
   Show that the derivative $f'(x)$ exists at every value of $x$ and that $f'(x)=f(x)$.
   
1. Graph the function

    $$f(x) = \sin x \sin (x+2) - \sin^2(x+1)$$

   What does the graph do?  Why does the function behave this way?  Give reasons for your answer.

1. Suppose that $y=f(x)$ is differentiable at $x=a$ and that $g(x)=m(x-a)+c$ is a linear function in which $m$ and $c$ are constants.  If the error $E(x)=f(x)-g(x)$ were small enough near $x=a$, we might think of using $g$ as a linear approximation of $f$ instead of the linearization $L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$.  Show that if we impose on $g$ the conditions
   * $E(a)=0$
   * $\lim_{x\to a} \frac{E(x)}{x-a} = 0$

   then $g(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$.  Thus the linearization $L(x)$ gives the only linear approximation whose error is both zero at $x=a$ and negligible in comparison with $x-a$.

1. *Schwarz's Inequality*
    1. Show that if $a>0$, then $f(x)=ax^2+2bx+c \geq 0$ for all real $x$ if and only if $b^2\leq ac$.
    2. Derive **Schwarz's Inequality:**
       
       $$(a_1b_a +a_2b_a + \cdots + a_nb_n)^2 \leq (a_1^2+a_2^2+ \cdots + a_n^2)(b_1^2+b_2^2+ \cdots + b_n^2)$$

       by applying what you learned in the previous part to the sum

       $$(a_1x+b_1)^2+(a_2x+b_2)^2+\cdots + (a_nx+b_n)^2$$

    3. Show that the equality holds in Schwarz's inequality only if there exists a real number $x$ that makes $a_ix$ equal $-b_i$ for every value of $i$ from $1$ to $n$.
  
1. You can estimate the value of a reicprocal of a number $a$ without ever dividing by $a$ if you apply Newton's method to the function $f(x)=(1/x)-a$.

   For example, if $a=3$, the function involved is $f(x) = (1/x)-3$.
     1. Graph $y=(1/x)-3$.  Where does the graph cross the $x$-axis?
     2. Show that the recursion formula in this case is

        $$x_{n+1}=x_n(2-3x_n)$$

        so there is no need for division.
